# Follow-up-paper references

This document collects verified primary sources for a *portfolio-level*
follow-up-paper research skill: one that reads a completed or ongoing **main**
paper — its thesis, results, verdicts, limitations, and open questions — and
proposes the next **application** papers to write. An application paper takes the
main paper's core mechanism and applies it in a new domain (e.g., the main paper
establishes that a layer can enforce *expressive hard monotonicity*; a follow-up
applies it to physics-informed neural networks so a PDE solution is admissible by
construction). The skill proposes candidate papers, prioritizes them by
impact/feasibility/fit/venue, and on acceptance scaffolds a new
application-paper project. It is the fourth of a set: the confirmatory half lives
in [methodology-references.md](methodology-references.md) (pre-registration,
strong inference, severity, `rliable`, equivalence, reproducibility); generating
hypotheses *within* a paper — including analogical transfer (Gentner 1983) — lives
in [hypothesis-generation-references.md](hypothesis-generation-references.md); and
assembling a paper's claim→evidence ledger lives in
[paper-synthesis-references.md](paper-synthesis-references.md). This file does
**not** repeat those; it cross-links them and instead grounds the choice and
framing of whole follow-up *papers* and a research agenda. Because the output
feeds a methodology section and the skill's design, citations must be real and
verified — every entry below was checked against its publisher of record, a
stable archive, or the authoritative institutional page; none is marked
UNVERIFIED.

## 1. Research programmes — how a core result spawns a protective belt of follow-on work

- **Lakatos, I. (1970).** *Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific
  Research Programmes.* In I. Lakatos & A. Musgrave (Eds.), *Criticism and the
  Growth of Knowledge* (pp. 91–196). Cambridge University Press.
  [Cambridge Core chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/criticism-and-the-growth-of-knowledge/falsification-and-the-methodology-of-scientific-research-programmes/B1AAD974814D6E7BF35E6449691AA58F);
  volume ISBN 978-0-521-09623-2.
  Key takeaway: A research programme has an irrefutable-by-decision **hard core**
  surrounded by a **protective belt** of auxiliary hypotheses; the **negative
  heuristic** forbids aiming refutations at the core, while the **positive
  heuristic** directs how to build and adjust the belt. A programme is
  *progressive* when its shifts predict novel facts and *degenerating* when it
  only accommodates known ones after the fact.
  Skill use: The core organizing metaphor for the skill — the main paper's central
  mechanism is the hard core, and each proposed application paper is a
  protective-belt extension. The skill should prefer *progressive* proposals
  (those that predict a new admissible result in a new domain) over *degenerating*
  ones (mere restatements), which is the mechanism-transfer generation lens.

## 2. Use-inspired / application-driven basic research

- **Stokes, D. E. (1997).** *Pasteur's Quadrant: Basic Science and Technological
  Innovation.* Brookings Institution Press. ISBN 978-0-8157-8177-6 (0-8157-8177-6);
  [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/books/pasteurs-quadrant/).
  Key takeaway: Rejects the linear basic→applied dichotomy for a 2×2 of
  "quest for fundamental understanding" × "consideration of use." **Pasteur's
  quadrant** (high on both) is *use-inspired basic research* — work that advances
  theory precisely *because* it is pursued in an application.
  Skill use: The theoretical justification for the whole enterprise — application
  papers are not a lesser activity but the quadrant where a mechanism's
  fundamental scope and its practical value are established together. Frames the
  "fit" prioritization axis: prefer applications that both extend understanding of
  the mechanism and deliver domain use.

## 3. Choosing which problem/paper to work on next

- **Hamming, R. W. (1986).** *You and Your Research.* Talk delivered at Bellcore
  (Bell Communications Research), 7 March 1986; transcribed by J. F. Kaiser.
  Stable archive:
  [University of Virginia CS](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html)
  ([PDF](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.pdf)).
  Key takeaway: Great work comes from working on *important problems* with an
  *available line of attack* — "it is not sufficient that a problem be important;
  it must be attackable." Keep a list of the important problems in your field and
  match them to newly opened doors.
  Skill use: Grounds the two-factor gate at the heart of prioritization —
  importance × attackability. The skill maintains the main paper's open questions
  as the "important problems" list and only promotes an application when the
  mechanism gives a concrete line of attack (an available method, dataset, or
  domain hook).

- **Alon, U. (2009).** *How to Choose a Good Scientific Problem.* Molecular Cell
  35(6): 726–728. DOI: [10.1016/j.molcel.2009.09.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2009.09.013);
  [author PDF](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/mcb/alon/sites/mcb.UriAlon/files/uploads/nurturing/howtochoosegoodproblem.pdf).
  Key takeaway: Introduces the **feasibility–interest diagram** — plot candidate
  projects on feasibility × interest and choose from the high-feasibility,
  high-interest region — and stresses that problem *selection* deserves months of
  explicit deliberation, not a snap decision.
  Skill use: The direct template for the skill's candidate-ranking scatter:
  feasibility ≈ (main-paper mechanism maturity + domain data/tooling readiness),
  interest ≈ impact + venue fit. The skill emits candidates positioned on these
  axes rather than a flat list.

## 4. R&D portfolio selection — exploration vs. exploitation under uncertainty

- **March, J. G. (1991).** *Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational
  Learning.* Organization Science 2(1): 71–87.
  DOI: [10.1287/orsc.2.1.71](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2.1.71).
  Key takeaway: Adaptive systems must balance **exploration** (new possibilities,
  uncertain and delayed payoff) against **exploitation** (refining known
  certainties, reliable near-term payoff); over-refining exploitation is
  effective short-term but self-destructive long-term because it starves the
  variance needed for future gains.
  Skill use: The formal frame for portfolio balance across a *slate* of proposed
  application papers — the skill should not return only safe near-core
  exploitations; it must reserve budget for at least one exploratory, farther-from-
  core application, and surface each candidate's exploration/exploitation posture
  as part of prioritization.

## 5. Mechanism / technology transfer across domains as a source of application papers

The analogical-transfer mechanism itself (structure-mapping) is covered by
**Gentner, D. (1983)** in
[hypothesis-generation-references.md](hypothesis-generation-references.md) §5 and
is not repeated here; the sources below add the innovation/translational side —
how transferring a working mechanism into a new domain is itself a recognized,
high-value engine of new work.

- **Hargadon, A., & Sutton, R. I. (1997).** *Technology Brokering and Innovation
  in a Product Development Firm.* Administrative Science Quarterly 42(4): 716–749.
  DOI: [10.2307/2393655](https://doi.org/10.2307/2393655).
  Key takeaway: Innovation at a leading design firm arises from **technology
  brokering** — carrying solutions that are routine in one domain into a second
  domain where they are novel, then recombining them. Creativity is largely the
  cross-domain *transfer and recombination* of existing mechanisms.
  Skill use: Empirical warrant for the skill's primary generation lens
  (mechanism-transfer): treat the main paper's mechanism as the "solution" to
  broker into domains where it is unknown, and rank a proposed application by how
  novel the mechanism is *in the target domain* while being proven in the source.

- **Butler, D. (2008).** *Translational research: crossing the valley of death.*
  Nature 453(7197): 840–842. DOI: [10.1038/453840a](https://doi.org/10.1038/453840a).
  Key takeaway: A "valley of death" separates a fundamental result from its
  applied payoff; promising mechanisms routinely stall for want of deliberate,
  resourced translational follow-through into application settings.
  Skill use: Motivates the skill's very existence and its feasibility discounting —
  an application proposal must budget for the crossing (domain data, baselines,
  integration), and the skill should down-rank transfers that have no realistic
  path across the valley.

## 6. Framing a proposed project's contribution, feasibility, and impact — the Heilmeier Catechism

- **Heilmeier, G. H. — DARPA.** *The Heilmeier Catechism.* Defense Advanced
  Research Projects Agency (authoritative institutional page):
  <https://www.darpa.mil/about/heilmeier-catechism>. (Heilmeier directed DARPA
  1975–1977; the catechism is the set of questions he asked of proposed
  programs.)
  Key takeaway: Eight questions to vet any research proposal — (1) What are you
  trying to do? (no jargon); (2) How is it done today, and what are the limits of
  current practice?; (3) What is new in your approach and why will it succeed?;
  (4) Who cares — if you succeed, what difference does it make?; (5) What are the
  risks?; (6) How much will it cost?; (7) How long will it take?; (8) What are the
  mid-term and final "exams" to check for success?
  Skill use: The template the skill uses to *frame* each accepted candidate and to
  populate the scaffold — every proposed application paper is instantiated as
  answers to the eight questions, giving contribution (Q1–Q4), feasibility/cost/
  timeline (Q5–Q7), and success criteria (Q8) that seed the new project's
  strategy doc.

## 7. The "adjacent possible" — which applications are reachable next

- **Kauffman, S. A. (2000).** *Investigations.* Oxford University Press.
  ISBN 978-0-19-512104-9 (0-19-512104-X);
  [OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/investigations-9780195121056).
  Key takeaway: The **adjacent possible** is the set of states one step away from
  what currently exists; systems expand by moving into it. Novelty is generated
  not by arbitrary leaps but by combining what is already actual into its
  immediate neighbors.
  Skill use: A model for the reachability filter on candidate applications —
  prefer domains that are *adjacent* to the main paper's mechanism (one clear
  transfer step: shared math, an available benchmark, a compatible constraint)
  over distant domains requiring several unproven bridges. Formalizes why "feasible"
  applications cluster near the mechanism's current frontier.

## How this maps to the skill

**Generation lenses.** The skill reads the main paper and emits candidate
follow-up papers via three lenses, each grounded above:

- *Mechanism-transfer* — broker the main paper's core mechanism (hard core,
  Lakatos 1970) into a new domain where it is novel but proven in the source
  (Hargadon & Sutton 1997), by structural analogy (Gentner 1983, companion file),
  preferring targets in the mechanism's **adjacent possible** (Kauffman 2000).
- *Limitation-driven* — turn a stated limitation or scope boundary of the main
  paper into an application that a different domain's constraints make tractable
  (progressive problemshift, Lakatos 1970; use-inspired framing, Stokes 1997).
- *Result-driven* — take a headline result or verdict and ask which application
  domain most needs exactly that guarantee (Pasteur's-quadrant use-inspiration,
  Stokes 1997).

**Prioritization (impact / feasibility / fit / venue).** Candidates are scored,
not listed flat: importance × attackability (Hamming 1986) and the feasibility ×
interest diagram (Alon 2009) set the two primary axes; *fit* is the
Pasteur's-quadrant test that the application advances both mechanism understanding
and domain use (Stokes 1997); the slate is balanced for exploration vs.
exploitation so it is not all near-core safe bets (March 1991); and reachability
discounts transfers with no path across the valley of death (Butler 2008) or that
lie outside the adjacent possible (Kauffman 2000). Progressive-over-degenerating
(Lakatos 1970) is the tie-breaker: prefer proposals that predict a *new* admissible
result over those that merely restate the core.

**Accept → scaffold.** On acceptance, the skill frames the chosen application
paper as answers to the **Heilmeier Catechism** (DARPA) — Q1–Q4 fix the
contribution, Q5–Q7 the feasibility/cost/timeline, Q8 the success "exams" — and
scaffolds a new project whose pre-experiment strategy doc is seeded from those
answers. That strategy doc then hands off to the confirmatory tooling in
[methodology-references.md](methodology-references.md), while any within-paper
hypotheses use [hypothesis-generation-references.md](hypothesis-generation-references.md)
and the paper's claim ledger uses
[paper-synthesis-references.md](paper-synthesis-references.md).
